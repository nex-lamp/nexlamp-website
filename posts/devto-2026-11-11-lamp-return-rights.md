On November 4, a complaint appeared on China's Sina Black Cat complaint platform. A consumer bought a ceiling light during the Double 11 sales period, had it installed, and found it could not produce white light. The installer told her to "try it yourself in the app" and left. After days of back-and-forth, the seller's final answer was one line: **"No returns after installation."**

Versions of this story spike every year right after Double 11. A luminaire is not a smartphone: ten minutes of unboxing will verify most of a phone, but a ceiling light only reveals its true character once it is wired in, powered on, and placed inside your room's actual light environment — that is when wrong color temperature, flicker, buzzing drivers, or failed dimming show up. By then you are past the seven-day window and, conveniently, "already installed."

Sellers count on that gap. But all three of their standard refusal lines fail once you know which rule actually applies.

## Excuse 1: "No returns after installation" — conflating two different types of returns

Chinese law separates returns into two tracks with completely different rules.

**No-reason returns** (you changed your mind): for remote purchases — online, TV, phone, mail order — you may return within seven days of receipt without giving a reason, under Article 25 of the Law on the Protection of Consumer Rights and Interests, provided the goods remain intact.

**Quality-defect returns**: governed by Article 24 — if goods fail to meet quality requirements, the consumer may demand return, replacement, or repair. This track is **not capped at seven days**: you can return within seven days, and after seven days you can still demand repair or replacement, and where the defect makes normal use impossible, rescind the contract and return the goods entirely. For quality-defect returns, the necessary transport costs are borne by the seller.

The "no returns after installation" line bundles a quality defect into the no-reason track's restrictions. Wrong color temperature, dimming failure, visible flicker — these are quality problems. The seven-day no-reason framework simply does not govern them. The China Consumers Association has stated this plainly: quality issues follow the "three guarantees" regime and are not constrained by the seven-day no-reason rule.

## Excuse 2: "It's been unsealed, so no return" — unsealing is not damage

For a lamp, the inspection process itself involves unsealing: opening the box, powering it on, testing color modes. Sellers use "you opened it, you installed it" to declare the goods damaged.

The CCA's position: **opening packaging for inspection purposes, or reasonable testing to confirm quality and function, does not affect the goods' "intact" status**. "Intact" means the product retains its original quality and function, with the item, accessories, and labels complete — unsealing and intactness are separate questions. A seller may not refuse a seven-day no-reason return solely because the package was opened, and certainly may not use it to block a quality-defect claim.

## Excuse 3: "More than seven days, no return" — the seven days govern "no reason" only

This is the most widely misunderstood line. The seven-day clock governs **no-reason** returns. Quality defects follow the three-guarantee regime: return or replacement within seven days, repair or replacement after that, and where the failure is serious enough to defeat normal use, statutory contract rescission and return are still available.

One documented complaint against a NVC (Leishi) flagship store: a lamp ordered during Double 11 was delayed-shipped and only signed for on December 23; the buyer never opened the box, discovered quality problems at installation on January 10, and the seller refused the return citing "past seven days." The clock runs from the day after signed receipt — mechanically applying "over seven days" to that timeline does not stand.

## The strongest card: the six-month burden-of-proof reversal

Few buyers know Article 23(3) of the same law: for **durable goods** — motor vehicles, computers, TVs, refrigerators, air conditioners, washing machines and the like — if a defect is discovered within six months of acceptance and a dispute arises, **the operator (seller) bears the burden of proof**.

In plain terms: for an electrical durable like a luminaire, if a problem appears within six months of installation, you do not have to prove "it left the factory defective" — the seller has to prove it did not. Many disputes stall on "prove the defect is not caused by your installation"; this clause pushes that burden back onto the seller. It is the provision to cite first, proactively, in any complaint or lawsuit.

## Four protective steps for delivery week

Legal clauses are the fallback; evidence is the first move. When Double 11 lamps arrive:

| Step | Action | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Before signing | Sign only if the outer box is intact; photograph or refuse damaged boxes | Damage liability sits with the carrier; hard to prove after signing |
| At unboxing | Record video the whole way: packaging, accessories, the lamp body | The standard play recommended by the CCA and local market regulators |
| At installation | Accept on-site before the installer leaves: test every color mode, every brightness step | "Try it in the app yourself" is exactly how the November 4 complaint began |
| Confirming receipt | Light up and check every fixture, then tap "confirm receipt" | The seven-day clock starts the day after signing — do not spend it on "not yet installed" |

The last step matters most: on e-commerce platforms, "confirm receipt" often marks the boundary of the return window and after-sales leverage. Not installed, not verified — do not confirm yet.

## If it comes to a dispute: three escalation tiers

1. **Platform intervention**: file the return selecting "quality problem," attach the unboxing video, fault photos, and chat logs. Most disputes end here.
2. **Administrative complaint**: call 12315 or file on the national 12315 platform; the CCA's "315" mini-program also works.
3. **Litigation**: for large amounts, with the six-month burden-of-proof reversal in hand, the cost of suing is lower than most people assume.

## FAQ

**Can I also claim the installation fee?**
For quality-defect returns the seller bears necessary costs; extra disassembly and return-shipping expenses can be claimed together in the complaint.

**Should I accept a replacement instead of a refund?**
Yes, but watch one trap: some sellers route replacements as "place a new order, refund the old one," and the price difference becomes a fight afterward (the November 4 complaint stalled exactly there). Get the difference and any coupon handling confirmed in writing, not verbally.

**What if the problem shows up much later?**
Within six months the burden of proof sits with the seller — that is the window. After six months you can still invoke the three guarantees, but the difficulty shifts back to you, so report faults early and keep records.

In this industry, good and bad products often share the same marketing photos. The difference shows in the sentence a seller uses after something breaks. This delivery week: record the unboxing, verify on-site, go slow on that "confirm receipt" button. Ten minutes of discipline buys back hundreds of yuan in fees and a month of wrangling.
