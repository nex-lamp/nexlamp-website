A homeowner in Jiangsu, China posted a video last month that will feel painfully familiar to anyone who has ever batch-bought lighting: a case of ten LED downlights he'd purchased during a mid-year sale, left sealed in the box for an entire quarter. When he finally opened it to install them during the autumn shopping season, all ten were dead.

The comment section split into three camps — the courier was too rough, the batch was defective, and (most tellingly) one installation company chimed in to say they'd heard the same complaint from contractors about that exact model: "arrives broken out of the box."

To be fair, once a package has sat unopened for more than three months, there's no way to prove where in the chain the damage happened. But the interesting lesson here isn't about assigning blame. It's this: **lamps rarely fail in the way you can see at the moment of unboxing.** The failures that actually cost you money are the ones that show up later — sometimes days after everything is installed and sealed away above your head.

## The real problem: damage that waits until the light is in the ceiling

An LED fixture is really two products in one: the LED board, and the driver (the power supply). If an LED chip is dead, you'll know the second you power it up. Drivers don't work that way.

A driver is a densely packed circuit board — electrolytic capacitors, a transformer, power semiconductors, dozens to over a hundred solder joints, and several plug-in connectors. It fears exactly two things: heat, and vibration.

Shipping is a long vibration test. That journey typically produces one of three kinds of hidden damage:

| Hidden fault | Mechanism | When it shows up |
|---|---|---|
| Cracked cold solder joints | Weak or starved solder joints work loose under vibration, raising contact resistance | Poor contact after warm-up, hours to tens of hours in |
| Loose plug-in connectors | The board-to-driver connectors get vibrated into a half-unseated state | Intermittent on/off, unexplained flicker |
| Shifted capacitors | An electrolytic capacitor tips over and presses against its pads | Fine at power-on, drifts out of spec once heat builds up |

Notice the common thread: **none of these fail on the spot.** You click the light on at your desk for ten seconds, it lights up, you call it good. Then it goes into the ceiling. Three days later, one fixture starts flickering — or just dies.

At that point, you're not just paying for a replacement bulb.

## Finding a fault before installation and after it are two different price tags

Here's something most buyers don't know: factories already run a burn-in step. Whole batches of lamps are lit continuously on racks, and the units that fail early are screened out before boxing. In reliability engineering this is called "leaving failures at the factory" — swapping a driver on a production line takes five minutes.

But shipping partially resets that screening. Vibration is a stress the burn-in room never applies. So the moment a lamp reaches you, it's effectively rolling off a *second* production line — and this time, the quality inspector is you.

| | Found before installation | Found after installation |
|---|---|---|
| How you handle it | Refuse delivery or use the no-questions-asked return window, shipping insurance covers the cost | File a warranty claim, wait for a technician, possibly past the return window |
| Disassembly cost | Zero | Pop out panels on a suspended grid ceiling; drywall ceilings are far worse |
| Proving your case | You have an unboxing video; responsibility is clear | "It worked fine before" — good luck proving anything |

Same broken lamp, one step different in the sequence, and the cost is off by an order of magnitude.

## The three-step inspection: one evening, done

**Step 1: Record the unboxing.** Film the entire process, starting from cutting open the shipping carton — capture the shipping label, the outer packaging, the inner packaging, and every fixture in full view. If a dispute ever happens, this is the only evidence that actually settles anything. Consumer protection agencies repeat this advice every major sale season for a reason.

**Step 2: Burn them in on a desk overnight.** Don't install anything yet. Set the fixtures up on a table, or hang them temporarily from a wire, and power them continuously for 24 hours (or at minimum, overnight). While they run, watch four things:

- **Flicker:** point your phone camera at the lit fixture. Full-screen rolling bands means the driver's ripple is out of spec — return it.
- **Noise:** buzz or sizzling from the driver. Get your ear close; a healthy driver should be nearly silent.
- **Brightness stability:** check periodically in the dark for sudden dimming, blinking, or a dark patch on one section of the fixture.
- **Smell:** any burning-plastic or scorched odor — cut power immediately and return it. No exceptions.

**Step 3: On/off cycling.** Thirty seconds on, thirty seconds off, for a dozen or so rounds. Weakly soldered connectors and the starter circuit are exactly what this stress exposes — units that survive the burn-in but die during cycling would have failed on your ceiling sooner or later anyway.

If you bought smart fixtures, add two more checks: re-pair one with your app from scratch, and flip the circuit breaker off and on once to confirm they recover cleanly after a power cut.

**Only when everything passes do they go into the ceiling.**

## What to return without arguing

Some failures shouldn't trigger a debate with the seller. Just return:

- **Cracked diffusers or bent frames found at unboxing.** Physical damage to the housing means return, regardless of "it doesn't affect operation."
- **Large-area flicker on power-up** (rolling bands on your phone camera). That's driver ripple beyond spec.
- **A fixture that went dark once during burn-in and came back.** Intermittent contact failure — install it, and you've signed up for the classic "works sometimes" script on your ceiling.
- **Color temperature clearly off from the label, or mismatched tint across fixtures in the same batch.** This is a binning problem, and replacements rarely match better.

The seven-day no-questions-asked return window and shipping insurance are standard protections when buying lighting in bulk during big sales — use them fully. Just remember one thing: **these windows start from the date of delivery, not from the day you finally get around to installing.** Box them unopened and discover the fault after installation, and you lose on both ends.

## FAQ

**Doesn't a 24-hour burn-in "use up" the lamp's life?**

No. LED fixtures are rated for tens of thousands of hours; 24 hours is negligible on that scale. What burn-in does is flush out the "infant mortality" units — the ones that were going to die in their first few dozen hours anyway. Those lamps wouldn't have survived the warranty period installed in your ceiling either, so exposing them early is pure gain.

**Can I leave the boxes sealed and inspect later, when I'm ready to install?**

You can, but don't let inspection slip past the seven-day return window. At minimum, film an unboxing to confirm nothing is damaged, power each fixture on once to rule out hidden driver damage, then put them away until installation day. Never unboxing at all means forfeiting your inspection window entirely — if they fail later, you're gambling.

**What if the electrician discovers a dead fixture on installation day?**

Photograph and video it on the spot, and have the installer note "dead on arrival" in their work record, then contact the seller for a replacement. This is exactly why running your own burn-in first matters — it moves the discovery step from "after the technician arrives" to "before," saving you a callback fee and a round of finger-pointing.
